The findings the pitch rests on#

Individual AI access changed time use, not the mix of tasks

A six-month randomized field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers, with a generative AI tool integrated into the email, meeting and writing applications workers already used. The 80% of treated workers who used it spent two fewer hours on email a week in the experiment's second half and reduced out-of-hours work. Apart from those individual time savings, the authors "do not detect shifts in the quantity or composition of workers' tasks resulting from individual-level AI provision".

What we use it for: separating individual time savings from changes in how work is organized. Shared agent knowledge is the intervention doublegate proposes; its effects require their own measurement.

Study scope: the tool tested was an office assistant. The experiment did not evaluate shared agent memory or establish why task patterns stayed unchanged. The World Economic Forum's September 2026 piece frames the same result as the "single-player" phase of workplace AI.

Dillon, Jaffe, Immorlica & Stanton, "Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI", AER: Insights (forthcoming)

Meta built a reviewed institutional knowledge system

Meta's "organizational second brain" uses adversarial review by an agent that shares no context with the author, validation that passes or fails rather than scoring, human expert sign-off, and a complete audit trail. They state they had not seen this level of validation rigor applied to a structured knowledge base without model retraining, and describe the output as "permanent, compounding institutional memory".

What we use it for: an independently implemented approach to turning expert corrections into reviewed knowledge updates. Its results belong to Meta's system; the article is not an evaluation of doublegate or a market survey.

Meta Engineering, 2 September 2026

Write-path review in our provider survey

Across fourteen providers we surveyed, a write is retrievable immediately. Veracity scores, confidence ratings and decay tiers change ranking — never visibility in the configurations covered by our survey.

What we use it for: the claim that this is a gap rather than a feature we are duplicating. It is our own survey, so read the method.

Public agent skills carry real security flaws

Of 3,984 public agent skills scanned, 36.8% had at least one security flaw and 13.4% a critical one. 76 confirmed malicious payloads were found, 8 still live at publication, and 91% of the malicious ones paired prompt injection with conventional malware. The barrier to publishing was a single file and a git repository.

What we use it for: why skills are gated on the same path as memories rather than trusted as configuration.

Snyk, ToxicSkills

Public registries exclude curation by design, and say so

The official registry for the standard agent-tool protocol commits itself, in its published design principles, to "no built-in ranking, curation, or quality judgments" and to "avoid features that require constant human intervention or moderation". Its ecosystem document expects that role to be filled by "subregistries [that] add value […] by providing curation".

Hugging Face, the most mature artifact registry in AI, scans uploads and states: "this is not 100% foolproof. It is your responsibility as a user to check if something is safe or not. We are not actively auditing python packages for safety."

What we use it for: evidence that a reviewed public library is an unfilled architectural role rather than a category we invented. Also a caution — these are deliberate choices with stated reasons (operational cost, vendor neutrality, liability), and anyone claiming to fix them inherits those costs.

A correction we made to our own case

A great deal of published material states that BCBS 239 requires data lineage. We searched the full 2013 text: zero occurrences of "lineage", "provenance", "traceability" or "audit trail". The document requires reconciliation with source systems and striving toward "a single authoritative source for risk data per each type of risk" — which is different, and weaker, than a lineage mandate.

The lineage requirement is real, but it lives in the ECB's 2024 implementation guide, which states lineage must let a bank "track back the source of the issue in a timely manner" and "allow traceability for (external) validation". That is the document we cite.

Why this is here: quoting the better-known document as a lineage mandate is the kind of borrowed authority a bank's second line is paid to catch. We would rather lose the stronger-sounding citation than get caught using it.

ECB Guide on effective RDARR, 3 May 2024

Findings that work against us#

These are on the site because leaving them off would make the rest less believable.

Independent reviewers are less independent than they look

A study of judge panels found that nine models across seven families produced roughly 2.2 effective independent votes. Correlated training makes correlated judgement, so adding reviewers does not add assurance linearly. Our design has to survive this rather than assume it away.

arXiv 2605.29800

None of 25 supervised banks had followed the principles fully

The ECB's own thematic review found that "none of the significant institutions in the sample […] including those classified as global systemically important institutions, had fully followed the BCBS 239 principles". Read one way this is our market; read honestly it is a warning — these organizations have large, long-running data-governance programmes, and we are one component inside one, not a replacement for it.

ECB Guide on effective RDARR, May 2024

Willingness to share knowledge falls as the audience widens

In a study of 80 professionals, people were "broadly willing to share these traces […] but only within bounded groups", and "comfort drops sharply as audience expands beyond close teams". The authors also found that the level of detail shared mattered less than who could see it.

What we use it for: it supports our author → group → organization tiering, and it warns that company-wide sharing meets resistance we have to design for rather than assert past.

Morris and Maes, MIT Media Lab — arXiv 2606.02763, 1 June 2026

Numbers we publish once we have measured them#

Four figures the category usually quotes from vendor blogs. Each has a measurement attached and a release it lands with, and until then it is not on this site.

  • Onboarding time. Measured on the first organization deployments: time to a new joiner's first productive agent session, with and without an inherited store. Published with release 4, the multi-machine release.
  • Cost per correction. What one reviewed correction costs against fixing the same wrong assumption separately in every private agent. Measured on the same deployments, published with release 4.
  • Gate accuracy. A labelled false-positive and false-negative set, run against a published poisoning benchmark, is the exit test for release 3. The catch rate and the false-alarm rate ship together, because one without the other is marketing.
  • Erasure. Decisions stay signed; the erased content becomes unreadable because its keys are destroyed, and the erasure itself is a recorded event. The schema carries the keys from release 1 and the tooling lands in release 4 — the compliance claim is made when an erasure request has been satisfied end to end, not before.

The rule we work to: if a claim reads like a snapshot of one machine, it is an anecdote and it gets labelled as one. Our own measurements — including the ratio of records to claims quoted elsewhere on this site — are illustrations, not population findings. The same applies to the front page's five hundred agents: it is a picture of scale, not a count of anyone's estate, and nothing in the argument depends on the number.

Research behind the problem#

Individual AI access changed time use, not the mix of tasks

A six-month randomized field experiment covered 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers. It added a generative AI tool to the email, meeting and writing applications they already used. The 80% of treated workers who used it spent two fewer hours a week on email in the experiment's second half. They also did less work out of hours. Apart from those individual time savings, the authors "do not detect shifts in the quantity or composition of workers' tasks resulting from individual-level AI provision".

What we use it for: telling time saved by one person apart from a change in how work is organized. The World Economic Forum's September 2026 piece frames the same result as the "single-player" phase of workplace AI. Knowledge shared between agents is the second kind of change, the one doublegate offers, and its effects need their own measurement.

Study scope: the tool tested was an office assistant. The experiment did not evaluate shared agent memory. It did not establish why task patterns stayed unchanged.

Dillon, Jaffe, Immorlica & Stanton, "Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI", AER: Insights (forthcoming)

Meta built a reviewed institutional knowledge system

Meta's "organizational second brain" uses adversarial review by an agent that shares no context with the author. Its validation passes or fails rather than scoring. A human expert signs off, and the audit trail is complete. Meta's engineers write that they "haven't seen this level of validation rigor applied to a structured knowledge base without model retraining". They describe the output as "permanent, compounding institutional memory".

What we use it for: a team independent of us built a way to turn expert corrections into reviewed knowledge updates. The article evaluates Meta's system, not doublegate, and it is not a market survey.

Meta Engineering, 2 September 2026

Two of fourteen memory providers can hold changes for review

Of the fourteen memory providers we surveyed in September 2026, two offer a review step before what an agent writes is final. Since version 3.14.0, Mnemosyne can hold everything written until it is approved. Byterover holds back two kinds of change from the rest of the team: a deletion, and a change its own curation agent rates high-impact. The author's own queries already see the change. In both, the agent that wrote a change can approve it, and neither records the decision as a signed event.

What we use it for: holding back what an agent writes is no longer rare. The difference left is who may approve, and whether the decision is signed. We read both providers' own documentation on 2026-10-06. The survey of the fourteen is our own.

Mnemosyne v3.14.0 release notes, "Write-Approval Gate" Byterover documentation, "Review Changes" Our survey of fourteen providers — method and per-provider notes available on request

More than a third of public agent skills had a security flaw

A skill is a set of instructions, sometimes with scripts, that changes what an agent does. Of 3,984 public agent skills scanned, 36.8% had at least one security flaw and 13.4% a critical one. The scan confirmed 76 malicious payloads, 8 of them still live when the study was published. Of the malicious ones, 91% paired prompt injection with conventional malware. The barrier to publishing a skill was a single file and a git repository.

What we use it for: why a skill is held, scanned and reviewed like anything else an agent writes, instead of going straight into use.

Snyk, ToxicSkills

Packed skill malware got past every scanner tested

The authors took 1,613 malicious skills found in the wild and packed each one. Packing hides the payload when the skill is installed and restores it when the skill runs. They tested eight scanners: six that inspect a skill without running it, and two that ask a language model to judge it. The packed skills got past every one more than 90% of the time, with the two model judges tried on a random 100 of the skills. Disguising the payload's code, instead of packing it, got past most of the six non-running scanners more than 80% of the time. The authors' own answer was to run each skill in a sandbox and watch what it does. It detected 97% of attacks at a 2% false-positive rate.

What we use it for: the gate's grading model reads a skill as it would be installed. That is the same kind of check as the two model judges the packed skills got past. So a grade of a skill proves no more than the scan does. Both hold a skill for review and never prove it safe, and review is not permission to run it.

Ji et al., "Cloak and Detonate: Scanner Evasion and Dynamic Detection of Agent Skill Malware", arXiv 2607.02357, July 2026

Public registries exclude curation by design, and say so

The official registry of the Model Context Protocol, the standard agent-tool protocol, publishes its design principles. In them it commits itself to "no built-in ranking, curation, or quality judgments" and to "avoid features that require constant human intervention or moderation". Its ecosystem document expects that role to be filled by "subregistries [that] add value […] by providing curation".

Hugging Face, the most mature artifact registry in AI, scans uploads and states: this is not 100% foolproof. It is your responsibility as a user to check if something is safe or not. We are not actively auditing python packages for safety.

What we use it for: by their own design, the registries leave review to whoever installs, for stated reasons: operational cost, vendor neutrality and liability. Whoever reviews instead carries those costs.

A correction we made to our own case

Much published material states that BCBS 239 requires data lineage. We searched the full 2013 text: zero occurrences of "lineage", "provenance", "traceability" or "audit trail". The document requires reconciliation with source systems, and striving toward "a single authoritative source for risk data per each type of risk". That is a different requirement from a lineage mandate, and a weaker one.

The lineage requirement is real. It lives in the ECB's 2024 implementation guide. The guide states lineage must let a bank "track back the source of the issue in a timely manner" and "allow traceability for (external) validation". That is the document we cite.

Why this is here: a bank's risk and compliance function, its second line of defence, is paid to catch borrowed authority. Quoting the better-known document as a lineage mandate is that kind of borrowed authority. We would rather lose the stronger-sounding citation than be caught using it.

ECB Guide on effective RDARR, 3 May 2024

Each rule, and how to check it#

The gate, the doublegate service on the operator’s machine, enforces five rules on each claim, one thing written down for reuse. Each row says how, how anyone can check it from what we publish, and any limit on one machine. The checks are for whoever runs the gate.

RuleHow the gate enforces itHow to check it
The reviewer is never the author. Before a promotion counts a signature, the gate drops any signer who is the claim's author, whether an agent, a person or the operator. In the console, the author sees Refused: the claim's author cannot sign it where Sign would be. If an agent starts as the gate's own operating-system user, its shell can promote what its session wrote (how to prevent that). Run the agent as another operating-system user and the rule holds against it. A gate that runs under a service user of its own is on the roadmap. No method an agent-role API key can call promotes, signs, rejects, demotes or hides, and no method at any role deletes. The published method catalog names the role each method needs: an agent-role API key reaches 37 of 83. The role table on the engineers' page counts every role.
Other agents can find a claim once it is signed. Recall, the gate's search, returns promoted claims, then the asker's own held claims after them, marked held. Claims by other authors that are still held are not searched. Unless the operator requires a person, both signatures a promotion needs come from one grading model, which reads each claim twice: once for it and once against. By default an agent recalls from its own work area, then each area above it. Recalling across the space searches every work area in it. Have one agent remember a claim, then recall across the space with another: the claim is absent until it is promoted. When its author recalls it, the answer carries the held mark, a field of every recall hit in the published OpenAPI.
Every decision is signed and kept. Each claim written, and each promotion, rejection and demotion, is an event in the journal. Each is signed with the gate's signing key and linked to the one before it, so a later change shows. Nothing is edited in place or deleted, so storage only grows. Run doublegate verify on the gate's machine, with the gate's home directory or an API key. It walks the chain and checks every signature.
Only its author and reviewers can read a held claim. Without the reviewer role, ask for another author's held claim by id, in a listing or in a count. The gate answers as if the claim did not exist. With an agent-role API key, ask for another author's held claim by its id. Then ask for an id that no claim has, and compare the two answers. A caller can still learn four things, timing among them, measured on the governance page.
Promotion makes a claim findable and never ranks it higher. A promotion adds no weight to a claim. Every promotion on one gate is marked provisional, checked by this one gate alone. The mark shows on each hit without moving it. By default recall matches words. An embedding model, if the operator configures one, adds matching by meaning. Read any recall answer: each hit carries the provisional mark, a field in the published OpenAPI, beside the search's own score. The mark does not change the order.

What we measured on one machine#

Figures were measured on 2026-10-05 on one WSL2 machine, an AMD Ryzen 9 5950X, unless a figure gives its own date. A measurement on one machine is an illustration. Each figure is the rate of one operation, and none of them is the capacity of a deployment.

The journal appends about 700 signed events a second

An event is one entry in the journal, such as a claim written or a promotion. The median time to append one signed event was 1.34 to 1.40 ms across the journal sizes tried, or 682 to 733 events a second. The rate held flat from ten thousand to a million events. Each event is on disk before the gate answers, and waiting for the disk is what caps the rate.

our own measurement, 2026-10-05

Checking every signature takes 56 µs an event

Verifying the whole journal took 56 µs an event: 56 seconds for a million events. The time grows in a straight line with the journal's length. The check holds no lock, so claims can still be written while it runs.

our own measurement, 2026-10-05

Each claim uses about 14.5 KB of disk, permanently

A promoted claim took about 14.5 KB on disk, 4.3 KB of it in the journal. Nothing is deleted, so that space is never reclaimed and storage only grows.

our own measurement, 2026-10-05

Copying the gate's home directory runs at 90 to 113 MB/s

Copying a 1.6 GB home directory, where the gate keeps what it stores, took 17.8 seconds. Backup comes with the Solo release, the next release of today's gate. A backup cannot run faster than a plain copy, so 90 to 113 MB/s is the fastest a backup can go. How long copying a running gate holds up writing to it is unmeasured.

our own measurement, 2026-10-05

The scanner takes 305 ms per MiB of text

The scan for secrets and injected instructions read prose with no findings at 305 ms per MiB, about 0.3 ms a KiB. The rate is from one development machine on 2026-09-23, down from 366 ms. Secret patterns now skip text that lacks the prefix every match must carry. The injection patterns have no such prefix and take about 61% of the time. A clean scan still leaves the claim held for review. It never proves the claim safe.

our own measurement, 2026-09-23

What a caller can still learn about another author's held claim

A caller without the reviewer role is answered as if another author's held claim did not exist. The four things it can still learn, timing among them, are measured once, on the governance page.

our own measurement, 2026-10-06

Search by meaning inside one work area misses most of the best matches

A search by meaning confined to a work area holding 1% of the claims found 0.165 to 0.195 of the true top 10. That is about one in six to one in five. The result held at every store size tried. It is a limit on correctness, not on speed.

our own measurement, 2026-10-05, against an exact search

How often the grading model is right is unmeasured

No experiment has counted how often the grading model promotes a claim it should have rejected, or rejects one it should have promoted. Until one does, a claim the model promoted alone rests on one model's judgement, read twice. One setting requires a person for any kind of content.

our own evaluation record, 2026-10-06

Findings that work against us#

Each finding below weakens a claim made elsewhere on this site, and each carries its source like the rest.

The grading model's accuracy is unmeasured

How often the grading model is right is unmeasured. A promotion it signs alone is that model's judgement and nothing more.

our own evaluation record, 2026-10-06

The first falsification experiment was inconclusive

The first falsification experiment was inconclusive. It asked whether content ranked down still reaches an agent's prompt. The signal it needed was absent from all 393 memories it ran over.

our own evaluation, 2026-09-06

Two readings from one model are one judgement

Judge panels of nine models from seven families give about two independent votes. Our two readings share one model, so they are not independent reviewers.

arXiv 2605.29800

Holding a write for approval is no longer ours alone

Two of the memory providers we surveyed offer a review step before what an agent writes is final. In both, the agent that wrote can approve it. Against those two, the difference left is who may approve, and whether the decision is signed.

Our survey of fourteen providers — available on request

A full quarantine refuses every write

At 10,000 held claims every write is refused. Third-party content held for a countersign stays held today.

our own reading of the gate's settings and measurements, 2026-10-05

No significant institution in the ECB's sample had fully followed the principles

The ECB's own thematic review found that "none of the significant institutions in the sample […] including those classified as global systemically important institutions, had fully followed the BCBS 239 principles". These institutions run large, long-running data-governance programmes and still fell short. A gate is one component inside such a programme, never a replacement for it.

ECB Guide on effective RDARR, May 2024

Willingness to share knowledge falls as the audience widens

In a study of 80 professionals, people were "broadly willing to share these traces […] but only within bounded groups", and "comfort drops sharply as audience expands beyond close teams". The authors also found that the level of detail shared mattered less than who could see it.

What we use it for: it supports sharing within a group first. It also warns that sharing across a whole company meets resistance a product has to design for.

Morris and Maes, MIT Media Lab — arXiv 2606.02763, 1 June 2026

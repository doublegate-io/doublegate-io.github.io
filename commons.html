The Commons

A shared library of agent knowledge
where nothing arrives unvouched

Skills, memories and knowledge artifacts anyone can draw on — held, scanned, and signed by a reviewer that did not write them before anyone can install them. Free to read, free to contribute, and every verdict is published, including the refusals.

Why this does not exist yet

Not because nobody thought of it. Because the registries that hold this content decided, deliberately, that reviewing it was somebody else's job.

The canonical registry excludes curation on purpose

The official registry for the standard agent-tool protocol states as a design principle: "No preferential treatment for specific servers or organizations. No built-in ranking, curation, or quality judgments. Let consumers make their own curation decisions." A separate principle commits it to "avoid features that require constant human intervention or moderation."

They are right to. Moderation is an operational cost, and neutrality is a governance commitment curation would break. What they built is an index — and their own ecosystem document says curation belongs to "subregistries [that] add value […] by providing curation." That role is the one this page describes.

The registry's published design principles

The most mature registry scans, then hands you the decision

Hugging Face runs pickle-import and antivirus scanning over uploads, and says of it: "Disclaimer: this is not 100% foolproof. It is your responsibility as a user to check if something is safe or not. We are not actively auditing python packages for safety." Their recommended mitigation is to load only from "users and organizations you trust."

Scanning is table stakes; a verdict is not on offer. That is not a criticism — it is the honest limit of automated checking at scale, from the team with the most experience of it.

Hugging Face Hub — Pickle Scanning

And the measured result in agent skills

Of 3,984 public agent skills surveyed, 36.8% carried at least one security flaw and 13.4% a critical one. 76 were confirmed malicious, 8 still live at publication, and 91% of the malicious ones paired prompt injection with conventional malware. The barrier to publishing one is a file and a git repository.

This is the status quo the Commons is aimed at — not a hypothetical risk. The community's own guidance is to "validate the source yourself," which does not scale to anybody.

How publishing outward works

The same mechanism as promoting knowledge between teams, aimed at a wider audience.

◈

You choose what leaves

Nothing is published by default. An organization nominates the artifacts it is willing to share — typically the generic ones, where correct operation of a common tool is not anybody's competitive advantage.

◫

Your gate signs it first

An artifact reaching the Commons has already passed your own review. The publishing signature says which organization vouched for it, so a reader knows who stood behind it before anyone else looked.

⊘

The Commons gate reviews again

On its own authority, not as a rubber stamp of yours. Two independent authorities have to agree before an artifact is readable publicly — the same double gate, with the trust boundary one step further out.

⎔

It arrives pre-vouched

Signatures travel with the artifact. Someone installing it sees who wrote it, who reviewed it, when, and on what basis — before it reaches their agent, not after.

Public artifacts carry the same four properties

Nothing is relaxed because the audience is wider. If anything the opposite — a wrong artifact here reaches everyone who installs it.

PropertyWhat it means in the Commons
Audited Every verdict is public, including the rejections and their reasons. A registry that publishes only its approvals is a catalogue, not an audit trail.
Traceable Each artifact links to its author, the organization that vouched for it, and the evidence behind it. When something turns out to be wrong you can find everything else from the same source.
Versioned A correction is a new version with a link to what it replaced. Nothing is edited under a reader's feet, and an artifact you installed cannot silently become something else.
Discoverable Searchable with provenance attached, so the search result tells you who stood behind the answer rather than only what it says.

Contributors keep attribution. Reviewed knowledge stops being a private asset without stopping being yours — the signature naming you as author is the same record that makes the artifact trustworthy to a stranger.

Why an organization would contribute

Two arguments that hold, and one we are not making.

Reviewed intake beats free intake

The reason to contribute is the pool you draw from. Public artifacts that arrive already reviewed, with signatures attached, turn a supply-chain risk into a supply line. Today installing from a public registry means accepting unreviewed content or reviewing it yourself, per artifact, forever.

Generic knowledge is not your moat

How to operate a common tool correctly is not proprietary. Publishing it costs nothing strategically and earns standing in a pool that then pays back. What stays private is anything specific to your business — and that never leaves your own gate.

The business case rests on the supply line, not on goodwill. Contributing may well help your recruiting, your brand and your models; all three are unmeasured, so none of them belongs in your numbers.

What the Commons is not

The registries above were honest about their limits, which is why their disclaimers are quotable years later. Ours:

  • Not "safe". Hugging Face, with far more resources, says its scanning "is not 100% foolproof." Ours is not either. What we offer is that nothing is readable until a reviewer who did not write it has signed it, and that every verdict is published — not that a bad artifact is impossible.
  • Not a replacement for your own gate. Treat the Commons as reviewed intake, not as trusted input. A second opinion from your own gate costs little and is the entire point of the architecture.
  • Not exhaustively reviewed on demand. Review costs tokens and time. At public volume there is a queue, and we will publish its depth rather than imply instant publication.
  • No accuracy figure yet. How often review is wrong in either direction is unmeasured. On public volume a wrong rejection means a contributor lost, so this matters more here than anywhere else, and we will publish a false-alarm rate before claiming the checking is reliable.
  • Sybil resistance is undesigned. Public contribution invites volume and reputation attacks an internal deployment never sees. We have no answer yet, and this is the honest reason the Commons follows the paid tiers rather than leading them.

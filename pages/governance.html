Connectors and governance

Your systems already know things.
Your agents don't.

The knowledge your agents need is sitting in a core banking platform, a warehouse, a CRM, a ticketing system. doublegate lets those systems propose what an agent should know — and puts every proposal through the same review as anything a person writes.

A system proposes, the gate decides

A connector reads a slice of a source system and proposes beliefs. It cannot write directly into what your agents read — nothing can.

◫

Scoped by contract

A connector declares which dataset, which fields, and which refresh cadence it may derive from. Anything outside that scope is refused at the boundary rather than reviewed and rejected later.

⊘

Reviewed like everything else

A proposal from a system goes through the same holding, scanning, grading and signing as a memory typed by an engineer. A connector is not a trusted writer — it is another author whose work needs a second party.

⎔

Traceable to the row it came from

Every derived belief keeps a link to the source system, the dataset slice, the fields in scope and the run that produced it. "Where did this come from?" resolves to a record, not a guess.

A connector is a new kind of author, and that means a new kind of risk: one with your organization's own credentials behind it. A misconfigured connector proposing thousands of wrong beliefs is exactly what review is for — and why a connector cannot be its own approver.

What a connector actually produces

Three shapes, from the concrete to the organizational.

From a dataset slice

A connector reads a defined slice — say a product catalogue, a fee schedule, or a reference table — and proposes the stable facts an agent keeps getting wrong. Reviewed once, they stop being re-derived from scratch by every agent that needs them.

What is recorded: the source system, the slice, the fields in scope, the run that produced it, the reviewer, and the signature.

From operational history

A ticketing or incident system holds the resolutions your team has already worked out. A connector proposes those as reusable knowledge instead of leaving them to be rediscovered by whoever is on call next.

What is recorded: which incidents a belief was derived from, so a wrong generalisation can be traced back and withdrawn.

From documented policy

Internal standards, approved procedures and conventions live in wikis nobody reads. Proposed through a gate, they become knowledge an agent holds and cites — with a signature saying who confirmed it is current.

What is recorded: the document version a belief came from, so knowledge goes stale visibly rather than silently.

A refusal produces this same record with a refused verdict. The record exists either way — a gate that only files its successes is not an audit trail.

⎔

Audited

Every approval and rejection is a signed record: who decided, when, on what basis, and what they were looking at. Including the rejections — a gate that only records its successes is not an audit trail. Nothing is ever silently dropped: reject, demote and expire are themselves recorded events with their reasons.

◈

Traceable

Every belief links back to the evidence it was derived from. When something turns out to be wrong, you can find what produced it and what else came from the same source.

⊘

Versioned

Nothing is edited in place. A correction is a new version with a link to what it replaced, so the history of what your organization believed — and when it changed its mind — stays intact.

◫

Discoverable

Approved knowledge is searchable by the agents entitled to read it, with its provenance attached. Content still under review is discoverable to reviewers only.

Speaking formats your governance team already has tools for

An audit trail nobody can import is an audit trail nobody reads. The records are designed to express in established vocabularies rather than a private format.

Provenance as a standard graph

Derivation records map onto the W3C provenance vocabulary — entity, activity, agent, "was derived from", "was attributed to". A settled W3C Recommendation, not something we invented for this.

W3C PROV-O

Lineage your catalogue understands

Connector runs are designed to emit OpenLineage events, the open lineage standard under the Linux Foundation, including its field-level lineage facet. Your data catalogue can consume our trail with no bespoke parser.

OpenLineage — Apache-2.0, LF AI & Data

One honest limit: a field-level mapping is clean for a direct transformation and genuinely hard for a generative one. When a model reads two hundred rows and proposes one claim, we can record the slice and the fields in scope — we will not pretend to a per-column derivation we cannot compute.

We sell the evidence, not the verdict

No product makes you compliant. Compliance is an assessment of your organization against a control framework — what we produce is the evidence that goes into one.

Anyone selling you the verdict rather than the evidence is selling something they cannot deliver. What we produce is what an auditor actually asks for: a signed record of who decided what, on what basis, and when. No regulator has certified, approved or endorsed this tool — nor could one, since compliance attaches to your organization and not to our software.

Two specifics, because precision here is the product. BCBS 239 does not contain the word “lineage” — we checked the 2013 text and found zero occurrences, so we cite the requirement to the ECB’s 2024 implementation guide, which is where it actually lives. Organization-gate reads use revocable keys granted access to named folders; administrative changes require separate authority. A folder selects a collection, while an access grant determines who may read it.
